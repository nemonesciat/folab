# Waikiki: lore additions (working draft, second session)

Builds on `waikiki-lore.md`; read that first. This note collects what a long brainstorming session added. Each point is
marked:

- **[decided]**: Nemo has settled it.
- **[agreed]**: Nemo liked it; treat it as the working version.
- **[proposed]**: suggested in the session, not yet confirmed. Fine to build toward, not to set in stone.

## Principles that came out of it

- **The Castaway is the Courier, not the Chosen One. [decided]** No faction can be joined and overthrown from the inside
  in an afternoon. Knowing a secret gives leverage over people, not a revolution. Some doors stay shut all game.
- **No super mutants on Oʻahu. [decided]** The danger comes from the island itself: a rich, overgrown jungle biome,
  mutated plants and animals, feral ghouls, and pirates. **[agreed]**
- **Not raider camps everywhere. [decided]** Settlements are differently hostile depending on whom they pay (see the
  enclaves below).

## The war's last hours, as they touch Oʻahu

- Canon: Anchorage retaken January 2077; Canada fully annexed; food riots and martial law across the U.S.; the
  government in hiding from March. The first warning on October 23 came from the **Pacific Fleet** (COMPACFLT),
  headquartered at Pearl Harbor. The bombs fell at 9:47 AM Eastern, which is **about 3:47 AM in Hawaiʻi**: Honolulu
  burned in the dark.
- Oʻahu's **trade winds blow from the northeast**, so fallout from Pearl and downtown blew southwest, out to sea, away from
  Waikiki and away from windward Waimānalo. A real reason the playable area is livable. **[proposed]**

## Waikiki

**The city [decided]:** cosmopolitan and open, a mix of all kinds of people, ruled by a king and an adviser.

**Words for the court:**
- *Kahuna* means a master of a skilled, often sacred craft (healers, canoe builders, priests). The high priest is the
  *kahuna nui*.
- The adviser who ran a kingdom day to day was the *kālaimoku* (Kamehameha's was Kalanimōkū, "the iron cable of
  Hawaiʻi"). That is the vizier.
- Warriors were *koa*; masters of lua (the Hawaiian fighting art) *ʻōlohe*; the officers who enforced the kapu for the
  chiefs *ilāmuku* (a good name for the kālaimoku's men).
- *Kahuna* as a title for masters of trades the kingdom relies on (kahuna of medicine, of guns, of boats, of the hunt)
  could be a modest rank the Castaway earns. **[proposed]** Becoming the kahuna (or kālaimoku) is a maybe, far down
  the line. **[agreed]**

**Seats of power [proposed: "Option B"]:**
- **The king in the Royal Hawaiian, the "Pink Palace of the Pacific."** Ceremony, feasts, legitimacy. (The Navy used it
  as a rest house for submariners in WWII.)
- **The kālaimoku on the grounded battleship.** The guns, the garrison, the pirate bargain. Tribute handoffs happen at
  the ship; those paid in tribute wait in the Natatorium's cells and the ship's brig. The king can say he doesn't know
  the details.
- **A kahuna nui at Diamond Head**, a temple in the old fort. Diamond Head's slopes had real heiau before they were
  destroyed. He makes the tribute holy: those sent away are offerings. **[proposed]**

**The strip, Kalākaua Avenue [agreed]:** Waikiki's answer to the New Vegas Strip: civilized, shops and businesses,
people dressed (relatively) well, where many quests start and end and much gear is sold. **[proposed]** places on it:
the Royal Hawaiian at the centre (its Lucky 38); the Moana as the grand inn where deals are made on the veranda; the
International Market Place, under its giant banyan, as the bazaar; the Duke Kahanamoku statue as the meeting point;
Fort DeRussy (an Army fort with a museum in a concrete gun battery) for the city watch or the gunsmith; the Hilton
Hawaiian Village's mosaic tower as the west gate, facing the ruins.

**The Ala Wai Canal is the strip's wall. [proposed]** Across it lies Waikiki's Freeside: the crowded poor who work the
strip and supply most of the tribute. The strip looks civilized because the far side of the canal pays for it.

## The battleship and the Natatorium

**Which ship [agreed]:** renamed from the Missouri to one of her **unfinished sisters**, completed in Fallout's timeline.
The model fits as-is (same Iowa class). Nemo is choosing between:
- **USS Kentucky (BB-66)**, recommended: about three-quarters built before work stopped, launched in 1950 only to clear
  the dock, later cut up, her bow grafted onto the USS Wisconsin after a 1956 collision. Islanders' nickname: "the
  Kentuck."
- **USS Illinois (BB-65)**: cancelled at war's end, about a fifth built. Sounds grander.

**Why she isn't at Pearl Harbor [agreed]:** brought back into service by a Navy stretched thin, she was **coming home
from the east** (the way ships from the West Coast approach Oʻahu, along the south shore past Koko Head and Diamond
Head) for resupply and crew rotation, an hour or two out of Pearl, when the bombs fell. **[proposed]** In the war's last
months she also served as an offshore martial-law headquarters, her brig a detention cell for rioters and "Chinese
sympathizers" (Hawaiʻi held Japanese-Americans at Sand Island and Honouliuli in WWII). Two centuries on, the
kālaimoku's tribute waits in the same cells.

**How she survived and grounded [agreed, details proposed]:**
- The downtown bomb went off about 6–7 km ahead of her, west-northwest. At that range (5–10 psi, for a megaton or so) a
  battleship's topside is wrecked but her armoured hull holds. A Pearl Harbor bomb alone, 16–18 km away, would barely
  have touched her. (At Bikini in 1946 the *Nevada* survived 23 kilotons half a kilometre away.)
- Holed at the bow and flooding, she was **beached on purpose** on the flat reef so she couldn't sink, as the *Nevada*
  was beached at Pearl Harbor in 1941. A ship run onto a flat reef settles **upright**: the stage stays level.
- **Where: on the reef off the War Memorial Natatorium [agreed]**, between Waikiki and Diamond Head, bow pointing west
  toward the home she never reached, in full view of the Waikiki shore and facing down the beach toward the Pink
  Palace.

**Her damage [proposed]:** the blast came from ahead and to starboard.
- Foremast bent or toppled aft, radars gone; bridge windows blown in, the pilot house gutted (the armoured conning tower
  intact); forward funnel caved in on its starboard side; mainmast down across the deck; starboard 5-inch mounts
  knocked askew; Phalanx domes gone; missile launchers crushed and burned out; the fantail crane toppled.
- The three main turrets whole. One barrel droops; Turret Two is trained hard to starboard, as if turning toward the
  threat.
- Starboard side scorched: paint blistered, teak burned, soot up the superstructure. **Flash shadows**, as at
  Hiroshima: pale silhouettes of railings and barrels, and one of a person, used sparingly.
- Bow holed below the waterline; the lower forward compartments flooded or sealed.
- Two hundred years: rust, bird droppings, plants in the superstructure, barnacles and coral below; reef fish in the
  shallows round her.
- The kālaimoku's additions: shacks and tarps on deck, a rebuilt signal mast with the kingdom's flags, a searchlight, a
  lookout nest on the stump of the foremast.
- **The guns are a bluff:** Waikiki believes they fire; the pirates aren't sure. Only one 5-inch mount has been
  restored; the 16-inch guns are dead.

**The Natatorium compound, a fortress enclave [agreed]:** isolated from the rest of Waikiki by Kapiʻolani Park and the
trees; the ship a fortress on top of that. Laid out like a castle **[proposed]**:
- **Kapiʻolani Park, the open ground before the walls:** mostly cleared so nobody approaches unseen; a few great
  banyans kept as lookouts. Ironwoods and banyans screen the compound from the city.
- **A wall** of rubble, sandbags and sheet metal round the Natatorium and its grounds. The Waikiki Aquarium next door
  as the garrison's quarters or larder.
- **The route aboard, through the Natatorium:**
  1. **The arch:** the kingdom's gate and checkpoint; the WWI plaque with its names still on the wall; where the
     kālaimoku speaks to the city.
  2. **The bathhouse:** guardroom, armoury, and the cells where tribute is kept.
  3. **The pool and bleachers:** a court of public judgment, the pool tidal, the crowd in the stands; the condemned
     become tribute.
  4. **Along the top of the side wall:** the pool is a sheltered boat basin for the ship's launches.
  5. **The seaward wall:** a timber pier on pilings starts here and crosses the reef shallows. One section near the
     ship can be raised or burned: the drawbridge.
  6. **A scaffold stair tower lashed to the hull**, salvaged pipe and timber with landings and switchbacks, up to the
     main deck. Beside it a **hand-cranked cargo hoist** counterweighted with her own anchor chain (for goods and
     prisoners) and **cargo nets** down the hull for those who aren't supposed to be there.
- Each step is less public than the last: city gate, cells, court, harbour, pier, ship.

## The pirates and the Flotilla

- **Its heart is a crippled cruise liner you can walk around [agreed]**, with the other boats round it adding to the
  pirates' weight. **[proposed]** Hawaiʻi had real inter-island cruise ships. Its staterooms hold captives before
  they go up the mountain.
- **Lashed round it [proposed]:** a container ship (stacked containers as housing, stores, cells), barges, tugs,
  fishing boats, yachts, gangplanks and rope bridges; **a floating dry dock** towed from Pearl Harbor, their real
  strength; **a market barge** at the edge, the one place land people trade.
- **Where [proposed]:** moored on the shoals of **Maunalua Bay**, outside the reef between Diamond Head and Koko Head,
  in sight of Waikiki and of the pirate hotels.
- **How it lives [proposed]:** water from the liner's desalination plant; fishing and the Huáng's food tribute; wealth
  from people (Waikiki's tribute, raids, castaways) sold to the Vault for guns and medicine; **glow divers** salvaging
  Ground Zero's drowned harbour. Captives are landed at night and marched up Pālolo Valley, along Waikiki's edge: the
  court letting them pass is part of the bargain.
- **Rule [proposed]:** each lashed vessel is one crew's holding; its captain sits on a council; the failing old captain
  on the liner holds it together. When he dies the lines may be cut.
- **Hurricanes [agreed: later]:** a lashed fleet would tear itself apart. The liner can't move, so it rides storms out
  on its heaviest chains (the most frightening place on the island); the small boats run for **Hawaiʻi Kai's marina at
  Kuapā Pond**, an old fishpond turned near-landlocked harbour; bigger ships scatter round Makapuʻu or stay. Two states
  of the world: the Flotilla full most of the year, thin and exposed in storm season while Hawaiʻi Kai overflows. In
  game, a script swaps boats at the Flotilla for copies in the marina. **[proposed]**

## Weather

- **Storms like Morrowind's ash storms [agreed]:** sideways rain driven by high wind. **[proposed]** approach: a
  slanted, streaked rain effect of our own (or an emitter kept round the player by script); high wind speed in the
  weather record so palms whip and clouds race; heavy grey-green fog, a dark washed-out image filter, short draw
  distance; roaring wind, rain on metal roofs, surf; lightning over the Flotilla and the ship. Gameplay: worse aim,
  people indoors, shops shut, boats kept in, and a chance to sneak where you normally can't.

## The Huáng

**Origin [agreed]:** defectors fleeing a China collapsing at the end of the war, on a **people-smuggling ship**
(*shétóu*, "snakehead"). Real precedent: the *Golden Venture*, a freighter that ran aground off New York in 1993 with
nearly 300 Chinese migrants aboard.

**Their ship [proposed]:** a tired coastal freighter, roughly 80–100 m, under a flag of convenience, its hold converted to
bunks. The captain drove her straight up the sand at **Waimānalo Bay**; two centuries of dunes have built up round her,
so she stands **half-buried and upright**, entered at ground level through a cut in the hull.
- Revered and stripped at once: the bridge, the radio room, the bell and the hold where the first generation lived (now
  the ancestral hall, tablets for the dead along the bunks) are kept with ritual care; the rest was cut away for metal
  over the years. Red paper and gold paint on the sacred parts only.
- **What still runs:** Waimānalo had a real sugar plantation; the Huáng grow cane and distil alcohol to run the ship's
  old generator (lights, irrigation pumps, the radio). The machinery and the skill to keep it are what everyone wants,
  and why the cane fields are guarded. (Alternative: a pre-war fusion plant still running: a bigger prize, a bigger
  target.)
- The name "Huáng": the captain's family; the ruling line descends from the smuggler.
- **Bellows**, the old Air Force station on Waimānalo beach, could be their stronghold. **[proposed]**

**The Ancestor and the dynasty [agreed]:**
- Over generations the smuggler captain became a mythical founder. 黄 (Huáng) is the same character as **Huángdì, the
  Yellow Emperor**, legendary ancestor of Chinese civilization, and the two blur: generation one knew the man who took
  their money; generation eight worships the Ancestor who read the Mandate of Heaven, chose who would cross, and founded
  a realm at the edge of the world. The bombs were Heaven's judgment; the voyage is the Crossing. (Like the retainers in
  *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* boasting of founders who ruled for centuries and kept out the barbarians, and Liu
  Bei's claim to Han blood.)
- **Older traditions restored, a natural hierarchy of families [decided]:** the manifest became the clan register
  (*zupu*); low numbers boarded first and are the elder houses (really, they paid the most); ship's offices became
  hereditary houses (the engineer's line keeps the machines, the navigator's the stars and calendar, the radio officer's
  the station and the archive); the captain's heir holds a title grown from *chuanzhu*, ship-master, toward a throne;
  the beached ship is the ancestral hall (*citang*).
- **Outsiders are barbarians [decided]**, after the classical Hua–Yi distinction (华夷之辨): the civilized inside the
  order, everyone outside is *Yi*. Waikiki's king is a barbarian chieftain playing at kingship.
- **The Koʻolau is their Great Wall [agreed].** Waikiki has a literal wall; the Huáng have a mountain range. Their
  isolation is a sacred duty, not fear. Trade at the gate is spoken of as barbarians bringing tribute, even though the
  Huáng are the ones paying the pirates.

**Their own people and the trade:**
- **They keep their people in the realm and teach them that staying is virtue [decided].**
- **Exile [proposed]:** the worst punishment in Waimānalo; everyone believes it means banishment among the barbarians.
  In truth exiles are walked to the gate at night and handed to pirate buyers. Only the Captain's house and a few
  officers know.
- **Outsiders who trespass [proposed]** are seized and sold: a barbarian has no place in the order, so it isn't a crime.
- **Pirates are not sold [proposed]:** the food tribute keeps the peace.
- **The cliff trail [proposed]:** Kaʻau Crater lies near the Koʻolau crest above sheer windward cliffs. A hidden trail up
  those cliffs to the crater's rim is the Vault's back door, unknown to the pirates. The Huáng traded there directly in
  the early years; now they mostly go through the pirates and keep the trail as a secret. That is why only they know
  what the Vault really is.
- **What they get [proposed]:** medicine and a little ammunition. Only the Captain's house hands out medicine, so
  healing comes from the dynasty: a mandate of Heaven in a pill bottle.

**Culture [proposed]:** Mandarin drifted over two centuries (a formal "ship tongue" among the elders, pidgin in the
young, old slogans surviving as idioms; on screen, pinyin without tone marks). The ship's bell their most sacred object.
The first generation buried facing west, toward China; their great festival Qingming, tomb-sweeping, at that cemetery.
The manifest read aloud on the anniversary of the landing, cargo numbers and all. Tai chi at dawn on the beach,
mahjong tiles of coral or bone, red paper over doors dyed with local plants, a patched lion-dance costume, mooncakes of
taro. Outsiders may trade at one gate market, never stay past dark, never climb toward the cemetery.

**Grounding in the real island [proposed]:** Cantonese laborers came to Hawaiʻi in the 1800s for the sugar plantations,
so the Mandarin-speaking newcomers landed beside a long-settled Chinese-Hawaiian community they didn't understand and
that didn't trust them (absorbed, wiped out, or a few ghouls who remember). *Pākē* is the old Hawaiian word for
Chinese, and can carry contempt depending on who says it.

**The truth under the legend [proposed]:** the real captain's log (a smuggler who overloaded the hold and put the sick
over the side; the beaching perhaps drink or mutiny, not foresight); the manifest's price column; a Vault-Tec receipt
signed with a Huáng seal. The radio officer's line keeps the true archive, which makes the old woman who runs the
station the most dangerous person in Waimānalo; perhaps playing the old songs is the price of her silence.

**The Castaway and the Huáng [decided: hard to make headway; details proposed]:**
- The Qing had words for it: *shengfan*, "raw" barbarians, and *shufan*, "cooked" ones. Raw: turned away at the
  passes, shot if you push. Cooked: let into the gate market, given the border work no Huáng wants, spoken to in a few
  words of English. Nothing higher: you can't join, marry in, or hold office.
- **An Asian character [decided: easier first contact]:** New Vegas offers Asian at character creation and dialogue can
  test race (GetIsRace). **[proposed]** Make it double-edged: guards hesitate (a returning exile's child? a lost
  line?), but once it's plain you don't speak the language or know the rites, some treat you with more contempt, a
  Huáng face with a barbarian soul. Faster to "cooked", a few doors that never open otherwise, the same ceiling.
- **Information isn't a key:** tell a farmer exiles are sold and he calls you a liar, reports you, or is broken by it;
  none of that makes him rise. Blackmail an officer and you get a favour and an enemy. Proof taken to Waikiki or the
  pirates shifts the island's balance; the Huáng order survives. Any real change in Waimānalo comes years later, from
  Huáng with their own reasons.

## The Vault

**The experiment [agreed]:** whether the rich will eat the poor. As far as could be found, no canon Vault was built
around cannibalism (nearest: Vault 11's yearly sacrifice, Vault 75's harvesting, the TV show's Vault 32 and its
mouse-utopia story). **[proposed]** details:
- Internal name **"A Modest Proposal"**, after Swift's 1729 satire suggesting the poor sell their children as food for
  the rich.
- The rich brought servants, staff and security who outnumbered them several to one. The food systems were built to
  fail after a few decades. The plans include a "protein reclamation" plant, cold storage far too large, a medical wing
  built like a butcher's.
- They did it slowly, with paperwork: ration "lotteries" that never picked the founding families, medical "transfers",
  a language invented so nobody had to say it. It became normal, then sacred. The founders are an aristocracy: the ones
  who are never eaten.
- When the servants ran out, the Vault turned outward and began buying from the pirates. Waikiki's tribute, the
  Huáng's exiles, and perhaps the Castaway all end at the same table.
- Who knows: the pirate captains who deliver, maybe; the Huáng elders by their trail; the kālaimoku, if he's ever been
  curious.
- Check the Vault's number against the canon list before choosing one.

## The hills and valleys: vassal enclaves

**Nemo's direction [decided]:** towns and factions the player can mess with; most pay tribute to Waikiki, some pay the
pirates instead, some neither; a source of people for the trade; differently hostile; ruled by vassals.

**The system [proposed], from old Hawaiʻi:**
- **Ahupuaʻa:** wedge-shaped land divisions from ridge to sea, usually a valley each. Each is run by a **konohiki**,
  the headman: the vassal.
- **Makahiki:** once a year the chief's people went round collecting tribute at an altar on each ahupuaʻa's boundary,
  marked with a carved pig's head (*ahu puaʻa*, "pig altar"). In game: **tribute season**, when the kālaimoku's
  collectors make their rounds and every konohiki must deliver food, goods and people.
- How a town treats you follows whom it pays (New Vegas's town reputations and faction disguises do the work):
  - **Loyal to the court:** orderly, safe by day, but they owe people at Makahiki, so a stranger counts toward the
    quota. The friendliest towns can be the worst place to fall asleep.
  - **Sworn to the pirates:** hostile to court colours, fine with traders, a crew in residence.
  - **Paying both:** paranoid, poor, hostile to anyone armed, ready to sell you to whoever comes first.
  - **Refusing everyone:** fortified, trapped trails, shoot first; trust earned slowly.
  - **Outside the system:** nobody bothers taxing them.
- **Danger without raider camps:** quota hunters hired by konohiki before Makahiki, looking for anyone with no
  protector (the Castaway above all); pirate press gangs on the coast; the jungle; the towns themselves.

## The Wild [agreed: jungle biome, island-born threats; specifics proposed]

- **Feral pigs** mutated into huge tusked boars (Oʻahu's mountains are full of wild pigs); prey for the hunters' lodge.
- **Wild goats** (a bighorner retexture).
- **Giant centipedes** (Hawaiʻi's real ones bite hard); caves, lava tubes, ruins. Needs a new model.
- **Rats and mongooses** (both brought to the islands): giant rats, a mongoose retexture.
- **Mutant crabs** on reefs and beaches (lakelurk-like); something worse in deep water, why nobody but pirates sails.
- **Feral ghouls** in the old valley neighbourhoods.
- **Mutated plants**: overgrown jungle, the arboretum in Mānoa.
- **The night marchers** (*huakaʻi pō*): in Hawaiian folklore, processions of spirit warriors on ancient paths at
  night; whoever sees them must lie face down. A Waikiki rumour of glowing figures on the old trails, never quite
  explained. Still living belief for many in Hawaiʻi: keep it rumour and atmosphere, not a monster to farm.

## Radio

**Wàngxiāng Diàntái, Homeward Gaze Radio (the Huáng) [proposed]:** run from the ship's radio room by an old woman of the
radio officer's line, the only one allowed to play the old records.
- Shanghai *shidaiqu*, 1930s–40s: Zhou Xuan, Bai Guang, Li Xianglan (Yoshiko Yamaguchi), Yao Lee, Gong Qiuxia, Wu
  Yingyin: the "decadent" music the state frowned on, the smugglers' quiet defiance.
- Hong Kong Mandarin pop, 1950s: Grace Chang (*Mambo Girl*, 1957): mambo and calypso in Mandarin.
- Instrumentals: Abing's "The Moon Reflected in Erquan" (erhu, recorded 1950); *Spring River, Flower, Moon, Night*; the
  *Butterfly Lovers* violin concerto (1959); folk tunes such as "Jasmine Flower" and "Kangding Love Song".
- In-universe originals: a paddy work song, a lullaby about the ship, one song nobody will explain that the old stop to
  hear.
- Between songs: tide tables and planting calendars; a pirate warning coded as weather; manifest names on
  anniversaries; **a numbers broadcast after midnight** (to the pirates? the Vault? no one?), a quest of its own.
- Licensing: most 1930s–50s recordings are probably still under copyright somewhere; traditional melodies are public
  domain, so new recordings of them are the safest route.

**Radio Atoll (an English beach station) [proposed, from the first part of the session]:** DJ Captain Coral, a
sunburned ex-lifeguard. Bobby Darin's "Beyond the Sea" (and Trenet's "La Mer", its French original), "Sea of Love",
"Harbor Lights", "Ebb Tide", Bing Crosby's "Blue Hawaii", "Sweet Leilani" and "Red Sails in the Sunset", "Bali Ha'i",
the Andrews Sisters' "Rum and Coca-Cola", Belafonte's "Day-O" and "Jamaica Farewell", Martin Denny's "Quiet Village",
"Hawaiian War Chant", "Sleep Walk", Rosemary Clooney's "Mambo Italiano", Doris Day's "Perhaps, Perhaps, Perhaps", Mina's
"Tintarella di Luna" (a song about a moon-tan: glowing), steel-guitar instrumentals; Dick Dale's "Misirlou" late at
night with the 1927 Greek original earlier in the day; "Aloha ʻOe" (Queen Liliʻuokalani, 1878) as the nightly sign-off.
Same licensing caution.
